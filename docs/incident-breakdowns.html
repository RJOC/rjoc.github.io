Incident Breakdowns — Retail & Manufacturing Cyberattacks

Full breakdowns of five UK-linked cyberattacks spanning 2017 to 2025: Musgrave, Marks & Spencer, Co-op, Harrods, and Jaguar Land Rover. Four of the five trace back to the exact same weak point — an IT helpdesk that trusted a phone call.

Timeline

  • 2017Musgrave's internal monitoring detects malicious software running inside its retail network, spanning the SuperValu, Centra, and Daybreak store brands.
  • Oct 2017Musgrave publicly discloses the incident, stating the malware attempted to extract debit and credit card numbers and expiry dates only — not cardholder names, PINs, or CVV codes.
  • Oct 2017No evidence emerges that any card data was actually exfiltrated. Musgrave nonetheless advises customers to check bank statements as a precaution.
  • Oct 2017The Irish Office of the Data Protection Commissioner and An Garda Síochána (Irish police) are notified; Musgrave engages a cyber breach response consultancy and applies technical fixes.

Attack chain

1

Network intrusion

Attackers gained a foothold inside Musgrave's retail network. The specific initial-access method was never made public.

2

Point-of-sale targeting

Malware was placed with the specific goal of scraping card numbers and expiry dates from payment terminals or the systems processing them — the signature of point-of-sale (PoS) card-skimming malware common in this era.

3

Narrow data scope

The malware targeted card numbers and expiry only, skipping cardholder names, PINs, and CVVs — consistent with card-cloning intent rather than full identity theft.

4

Detected before confirmed loss

Musgrave's own monitoring caught the malware and it was neutralized with technical fixes before any theft was confirmed.

Lessons learned

  • Card-present malware doesn't need your identity systems — it just needs a foothold near the payment path. Segmenting PoS/payment networks from the rest of the retail estate limits what an attacker can reach even after a breach.
  • Detecting malware before data leaves is a real, achievable goal, not just a consolation prize — Musgrave's outcome (attempt confirmed, theft not confirmed) shows monitoring paid off even without full prevention.
  • EMV chip and tokenization reduce the resale value of stolen magstripe-style card data, which is part of why this exact attack shape became less common as payment infrastructure modernized after 2017.
  • Notify regulators and law enforcement early even absent confirmed harm — looping in the Irish DPC and Garda immediately, rather than waiting for proof of loss, is the model.

Timeline

  • Est. Feb 2025Attackers are believed to have gained initial access to M&S's network weeks before the ransomware detonated — the intrusion sat quietly before being weaponized.
  • Apr 21, 2025Customers start reporting failed contactless payments and broken Click & Collect bookings in stores.
  • Apr 22–23, 2025M&S confirms a "cyber incident." DragonForce sends a taunting message to CEO Stuart Machin from a compromised employee mailbox.
  • Apr 24, 2025The DragonForce ransomware encryptor is deployed against M&S's VMware ESXi hosts and virtual machines.
  • Apr 25, 2025M&S pulls the plug on its website and mobile app, halting all online orders outright.
  • Late Apr 2025M&S confirms customer personal data was stolen in the breach; contactless payment and Click & Collect remain down in stores.
  • Jun–Jul 2025Online ordering is gradually restored after roughly six weeks dark. M&S guides investors to an approximate £300m hit to annual profit.

Attack chain

1

Vishing the helpdesk

Attackers phoned M&S's outsourced IT helpdesk, impersonating an employee to request account help.

2

Privileged MFA reset

The helpdesk agent reset multi-factor authentication on a privileged account without adequate identity verification — the entire chain starts here.

3

Credential access via NTDS.dit

With privileged access, attackers exfiltrated the domain controller's NTDS.dit file — the core Active Directory database holding password hashes for every domain user.

4

Offline hash cracking

Hashes were cracked offline, yielding cleartext credentials across a wide range of accounts — no further contact with M&S's systems required to do it.

5

Ransomware on the hypervisor layer

The DragonForce encryptor was deployed directly against VMware ESXi hosts and VMs — encrypting the virtualization layer itself rather than individual servers one at a time.

Lessons learned

  • A phone call should never be sufficient to reset MFA or credentials on a privileged account — require an out-of-band, verifiable identity check every time, no exceptions for "urgent" requests.
  • Segment the identity plane from the hypervisor management plane. One stolen NTDS.dit shouldn't be a straight line to encrypting every virtual machine in the estate.
  • Keep immutable, offline backups — and actually rehearse restoring from them, not just taking them.
  • Have a named, rehearsed crisis-communications plan. M&S drew criticism for slow, sparse updates while online sales sat dark for weeks.

Timeline

  • Late Apr 2025Attackers use the same helpdesk social-engineering playbook seen at M&S to obtain privileged credentials inside Co-op's network.
  • May 2, 2025Co-op discloses that limited member data was accessed and proactively shuts down parts of its IT systems — back-office and call-centre infrastructure — as a precaution following the attempted unauthorised access.
  • Jul 10, 2025The UK National Crime Agency arrests four people, aged 17–20, in connection with the M&S, Co-op, and Harrods attacks.
  • Jul 16, 2025Co-op's internal investigation concludes hackers actually stole the personal data of all 6.5 million Co-op members during the April intrusion — far more than the "limited" scope first disclosed.
  • H1 2025 resultsCo-op reports an £80m (~$107m) hit to operating profit tied directly to the attack.

Attack chain

1

Helpdesk vishing

Attackers impersonated a privileged user — the kind of target research typically points at a senior IT administrator — and called the helpdesk requesting a password reset and MFA deactivation.

2

Credential access, no malware

Valid privileged credentials were obtained without deploying any malware or tooling, keeping the activity outside the visibility of endpoint detection and response (EDR) systems.

3

Data exfiltration

The attacker accessed and exfiltrated the member database before Co-op could fully contain the intrusion.

4

Proactive containment

Co-op shut down back-office and call-centre systems itself — a fast, self-initiated move that likely prevented the ransomware-encryption stage seen at M&S.

5

Delayed scope discovery

The true scale — all 6.5 million members — only became clear after further investigation, revealed more than two months after the initial, much narrower disclosure.

Lessons learned

  • Co-op had run red-team and simulated-attack exercises beforehand — but real-world pressure let attackers bypass the very defenses those exercises were meant to harden. Preparation alone isn't proof against a good pretext call.
  • No malware means no EDR alert. When the entire "exploit" is a phone call and a valid login, detection has to happen at the identity layer — anomalous privileged logins, unexpected MFA-device changes — not the endpoint.
  • Initial disclosures may undercount scope. Plan legal and communications exposure around the realistic worst case, not the number confirmed on day one.
  • Fast, self-initiated containment can be the difference between a data-theft incident and a full ransomware-encryption event — compare Co-op's outcome directly against M&S's.

Timeline

  • May 1, 2025Harrods becomes the third major UK retailer hit within a week, after M&S and Co-op. Internal monitoring flags suspicious activity on the network.
  • May 1–2, 2025Harrods restricts internet access across all its sites almost immediately, containing the intrusion before data can be compromised. GCHQ's National Cyber Security Centre is looped in.
  • May 3, 2025Harrods confirms the attack was contained early, with no customer data accessed — the clearest "win" in this wave of UK retail attacks.
  • Jul 10, 2025Four people aged 17–20 are arrested by the UK National Crime Agency in connection with the M&S, Co-op, and Harrods attacks.
  • Sep 2025A second, unrelated Harrods incident: attackers breach a third-party supplier's systems, exposing data on roughly 430,000 customers. No passwords or financial data are included; Harrods attributes the breach to the supplier, not its own network.

Attack chain

1

Attempted intrusion (May)

Attackers attempt unauthorized access using tactics consistent with the same wave hitting M&S and Co-op; Harrods' internal monitoring catches it in progress.

2

Rapid isolation

Internet access is cut across all Harrods sites almost immediately, denying the attacker time to move laterally or exfiltrate anything.

3

Contained, no loss

The May incident closes with no customer data accessed — a rare clean outcome against this attacker wave.

4

Third-party compromise (Sept)

A supplier holding Harrods customer data is separately breached, entirely outside Harrods' own network.

5

Downstream exposure

Despite Harrods' own systems staying clean, roughly 430,000 customers' data is exposed anyway, via the supplier.

Lessons learned

  • Speed of detection and response genuinely changes outcomes — Harrods' May incident is the clearest evidence in this whole set that fast isolation can stop a Scattered-Spider-style intrusion before any data leaves the building.
  • Your security perimeter includes your suppliers. The September breach shows a well-defended core network doesn't protect customer data sitting inside a third party's systems.
  • Vet and monitor third-party data processors the same way you'd audit your own environment — "we weren't breached, our supplier was" is a distinction that means nothing to the affected customer.

Timeline

  • Weeks priorA vishing and spear-phishing campaign targets JLR's IT helpdesk and employees with elevated access — the believed foothold, consistent with the wider "Scattered Lapsus$ Hunters" collective's playbook.
  • Aug 31, 2025The intrusion begins; attackers move laterally through JLR's corporate network largely undetected.
  • Sep 1, 2025JLR discovers the breach and proactively shuts down its global IT systems to contain the intruder.
  • Sep 2, 2025JLR publicly confirms it has been "impacted by a cyber incident" and pauses production as a precaution.
  • Mid-Sep 2025UK plants at Solihull, Halewood, and Wolverhampton sit idle alongside overseas plants in Slovakia, Brazil, and India; staff are told to stay home and suppliers begin laying off workers.
  • Sep 16, 2025JLR extends the shutdown to at least September 24 as forensic analysis and remediation continue.
  • Sep 23, 2025The restart date slips again, this time to October 1.
  • Sep 29, 2025The UK government announces an up-to-£1.5bn loan guarantee via UK Export Finance to stabilise JLR's supplier base.
  • Early Oct 2025Phased production restart begins after roughly five weeks fully offline — reported as the most economically damaging cyberattack in British history.

Attack chain

1

Vishing / spear-phishing

A helpdesk password-reset pretext combined with targeted phishing of employees holding elevated access — the same social-engineering entry point used against M&S and Co-op weeks earlier.

2

Credential access

Attackers gained a foothold using fraudulently obtained credentials; some reporting also points to reuse of stolen third-party credentials (e.g. from Jira) seen in other campaigns by the same actors.

3

Lateral movement

The attacker moved through JLR's corporate network over several weeks, largely undetected, before launching the primary assault.

4

Escalation toward manufacturing-adjacent systems

The intrusion reached systems tied closely enough to production that JLR's only reliable containment option was shutting down IT infrastructure wholesale.

5

Impact: global production halt

Manufacturing stopped for roughly five weeks across six plants in four countries while forensics, remediation, and a safe rebuild took place.

Lessons learned

  • Helpdesk identity verification is the recurring single point of failure across 2025's biggest UK breaches — treat it as a hard control, not a soft process.
  • Segment corporate IT from manufacturing/OT networks. A corporate breach shouldn't force a full production-line shutdown as the only available containment option.
  • Audit and rotate credentials across every connected SaaS tool. Reused or stolen third-party credentials (e.g. Jira) were a recurring entry point across this actor collective's other campaigns.
  • Map single points of failure in the physical supply chain — weeks of downtime cascaded into supplier layoffs and ultimately a government-backed loan, showing how fast cyber risk becomes balance-sheet and national-economic risk.

Shared Pattern

2017 vs. 2025 — the attack shape shifted. Musgrave's incident was malware-first: code quietly placed near the payment path to scrape card data, caught by monitoring before any confirmed theft. Nothing about it depended on tricking a human being. Eight years later, none of the four 2025 incidents needed malware to get in at all.

The 2025 wave shares one entry point. M&S, Co-op, and Harrods' May incident all trace to the same identity-first playbook within roughly a two-week span in April–May 2025: call the helpdesk, impersonate a privileged user, talk your way into an MFA or password reset. JLR was hit by the same playbook months later. The UK National Crime Agency ultimately arrested four people (aged 17–20) on 10 July 2025 in connection with the M&S, Co-op, and Harrods attacks.

Severity was decided after the door opened, not by attacker sophistication. M&S had a path from stolen credentials straight to its hypervisor layer, so it became a ransomware-encryption event. Co-op contained itself before that stage, so it became a mass data-theft event instead. Harrods detected and isolated within hours in May, so it lost nothing — then lost data anyway in September through a supplier it didn't control. JLR's compromise reached close enough to production that a full IT shutdown was the only safe containment, turning an identity breach into a five-week manufacturing outage. Same front door in four of five cases; the blast radius was entirely a function of architecture and response speed, not how the attacker got in.
